Lead story

Plant roots carry a hidden "environmental mode" that drought switches on in more cells

A Nature study led by the University of Western Australia sequenced gene activity in hundreds of thousands of Arabidopsis root-tip cells and found that nearly every cell type splits into a group that switches on a shared set of environmental-response genes and a group that does not. Under water scarcity the share of cells in this mode rose from about 40% to about two thirds, and the shift depends on abscisic acid. Averaging methods had hidden the effect. This is Arabidopsis work, reported here from the university press release, not yet shown in crops.

    Plant roots hide a second identity to help respond to drought

    Single-cell sequencing of hundreds of thousands of Arabidopsis root-tip cells shows most cell types contain two states, one of which expresses shared environmental-response genes. Water scarcity raised the share of cells in that state from about 40% to about 67%, and plants unable to make or sense abscisic acid had far fewer. Arabidopsis only; from the press release.

    IIT Guwahati study reveals how plants adapt their water transport system during drought

    In Indian mustard under severe water shortage, xylem tubes narrow and pits shrink, raising resistance to flow, while sideways flow through the pits helps keep water balance. The authors suggest the findings could inform drought-tolerant breeding. Reported second hand via regional news.
